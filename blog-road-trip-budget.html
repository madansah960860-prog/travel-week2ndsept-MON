What a national parks road trip really costs

Two weeks, two people, five parks in the American Southwest, priced line by line in three styles: camping, mid-range and park lodges.

By Imogen Farrow Published 04/22/2026 Updated 08/02/2026 12 min read

A national parks road trip has a reputation as the cheap American vacation. It is cheaper than a comparable trip to Europe, but the gap is smaller than people expect, and the reason is almost always the same: the costs that are not obvious at planning time.

This is a full breakdown for a specific trip: fourteen days, two travelers, flying into Las Vegas and out of Salt Lake City, covering Zion, Bryce Canyon, Capitol Reef, Arches and Canyonlands. Roughly 1,450 miles of driving. All figures are planning estimates in USD and were current when this article was last updated; verify anything that matters before you book.

The big four lines

1. The rental vehicle

A mid-size car or small SUV picked up in Las Vegas and returned in Salt Lake City runs roughly $70 to $110 per day in shoulder season, before taxes and fees. Over fourteen days that is $980 to $1,540.

Then there is the one-way drop fee, which on this route commonly adds $150 to $300. It is the single most overlooked line in a Southwest budget. Flying in and out of the same airport eliminates it entirely, at the cost of a longer final driving day. On this route, returning to Las Vegas adds about 4 hours 30 minutes of driving and saves several hundred dollars, which is usually the better trade for two people.

Decline the rental company's insurance only if you have confirmed your own coverage. Many US credit cards provide secondary collision coverage on rentals, and many personal auto policies extend to rentals within the country. Confirm both before you are standing at the counter.

2. Fuel

At 1,450 miles and 28 miles per gallon, you need about 52 gallons. At an average of $3.60 per gallon that is roughly $187. An SUV at 22 miles per gallon pushes it to about $237.

Two adjustments matter. Gas inside park corridors and in small gateway towns runs well above the interstate average, sometimes by 60 cents or more per gallon, and you will buy some of your fuel there because you have no alternative. And idling in an entrance queue on a spring morning burns fuel without covering distance. Budget 15 percent above the arithmetic.

3. Lodging

This is the line that determines what kind of trip you are on.

Camping: in-park campgrounds typically run $20 to $35 per night for a site. Thirteen nights lands around $260 to $455 for two people sharing. The catch is availability: in-park sites for popular parks are released six months out and sell out within minutes. Dispersed camping on nearby BLM land is free and legal in many areas, and requires more self-sufficiency.

Mid-range hotels in gateway towns: $130 to $220 per night in shoulder season, higher in summer. Thirteen nights runs $1,690 to $2,860. Springdale, Moab and Bryce Canyon City are all expensive relative to their size because demand vastly exceeds supply.

In-park lodges: $200 to $320 per night, and they book roughly thirteen months ahead. Thirteen nights is $2,600 to $4,160. What you buy is location, which on a sunrise-driven trip is worth more than it sounds.

4. Food

Two people eating one restaurant meal a day and handling breakfast and lunch from a cooler will spend around $65 to $85 per day, or $910 to $1,190 over the trip. Cooking everything at camp drops it to roughly $35 to $45 per day. Eating all three meals out in gateway towns, where options are limited and priced accordingly, pushes past $150 per day.

A cooler is the highest-return $40 you will spend. Buy groceries in a full-size supermarket in Las Vegas or St George before you enter the park corridor, because the general stores in gateway towns carry a narrow selection at a significant markup.

Running total, before the surprises

Camping style: roughly $2,200 for two people over fourteen days. Mid-range: roughly $4,100. Park lodges: roughly $5,600. All three exclude flights.

The six costs almost nobody budgets for

These are the lines that turn a $4,000 estimate into a $4,900 reality.

  1. The one-way rental drop fee. $150 to $300 on this route. Covered above, and worth repeating because it surprises people at the counter rather than at booking.
  2. Timed entry and permit fees. Small individually, ranging from a few dollars for a reservation to a lottery fee for a specific hike, but they accumulate across five parks. Budget $60 to $120 for a two-week trip, and note that a lottery fee is charged whether or not you win.
  3. State parks that are not covered by the federal pass. Dead Horse Point, Goblin Valley, Valley of Fire and Snow Canyon all charge separately, generally $10 to $20 per vehicle. Budget $60.
  4. Laundry mid-trip. Fourteen days of desert dust means a laundromat stop around day seven. Budget $15 to $25, and roughly 90 minutes of your day.
  5. Gear you did not know you needed. A second gallon jug, a wide-brim hat, a better headlamp, more sunscreen, a tire gauge. Budget $80 to $150 and expect to spend it.
  6. The bad-weather day. Roughly one day in fourteen gets rained out, snowed on or closed. What replaces it is a paid indoor thing in a town: a museum, a long lunch, an extra motel night in a place you had not planned to stay. Budget $150.

Together these add $500 to $800 to a two-week Southwest trip. Add them to the running total above and the honest figures become roughly $2,800 camping, $4,800 mid-range and $6,300 for park lodges.

What the park pass actually saves

The America the Beautiful annual pass covers entry to every federal park site for one vehicle and its occupants. Individual park entry runs $30 to $35 per vehicle for seven days. Visiting five parks means you break even after three and are meaningfully ahead after five. Buy it, either online in advance or at the first entrance gate.

Note what the pass does not cover: campgrounds, timed-entry reservation fees, lottery permits, in-park lodging, shuttle fees where charged, and state parks. It covers the entrance gate and nothing else.

Where to actually save money

In descending order of impact for this trip:

  • Fly in and out of the same airport. Saves the drop fee and often finds a cheaper round-trip fare. Worth $200 to $400.
  • Mix camping and hotels. Camp in the parks where sites are obtainable, book hotels in the two towns where they are not. Saves $600 to $1,000 against all-hotel, without asking you to camp for two straight weeks.
  • Travel in the first two weeks of September. Post-Labor-Day rates in gateway towns drop noticeably while the weather stays excellent. Saves $300 to $600 on lodging alone.
  • Cut one park. Four parks in fourteen days instead of five saves fuel, a rental day and a lodging night, and improves the trip. This is the recommendation we make most often and the one people resist hardest.

What not to cut

Water, a proper spare tire and a check of the vehicle before you leave the last real town. Cell service is unreliable across most of this route, tow trucks are far away and expensive, and the desert is not forgiving of a plan that assumed everything would go right. This is not a budgeting decision.
