Your first international trip, start to finish

Everything a US traveler needs to handle before leaving the country, in the order you need to handle it.

By Teodoro Salas Published 02/04/2026 Updated 07/29/2026 13 min read

Roughly half of American adults have never held a passport. If you are about to take a first trip outside the country, the anxiety is rarely about the destination. It is about the paperwork, the airport, the money and the phone. This guide covers all four in the order they become urgent, with the specific numbers and timelines that matter.

Six months out: the passport

Start here, because everything else is faster than this.

A first adult passport must be applied for in person at an acceptance facility, typically a post office or a county clerk's office. You need proof of citizenship, a photo ID, a passport photo and the fee. Routine processing has run between six and eight weeks in recent years, with expedited service roughly two to three weeks, and both figures move. Check the current published times on travel.state.gov rather than trusting any third party, including this page.

The rule that catches people is validity, not expiration. Many countries require your passport to be valid for at least six months beyond your date of entry, and some airlines will deny boarding on that basis alone. A passport expiring in four months is functionally expired for most international travel. Look at the date now.

Some destinations also require a visa or an electronic travel authorization even for short tourist stays, and several regions have introduced new pre-travel authorization systems. Requirements change with little warning. Check the US Department of State country information page for your destination once when you book and again a month before you fly.

Three months out: trusted traveler programs

These two programs are frequently confused, and for an international trip the distinction matters.

TSA PreCheck speeds up departure security inside the United States. Shoes and belt stay on, laptop stays in the bag, and the line is usually much shorter. It does nothing on your return to the country.

Global Entry speeds up your re-entry into the United States through customs and immigration, using a kiosk instead of the main queue. It includes PreCheck. For international travel it is the better value of the two, because the arrival queue at a large US airport after a transatlantic flight is where the real delay lives.

Both require an application, a background check and an in-person interview, and interview appointments are the bottleneck. Slots at busy airports can be months out, though enrollment on arrival at some airports lets you complete the interview when you land from an international trip. Apply early, and if you already hold a credit card that reimburses the application fee, use it.

If you are traveling as a family, note that children under a certain age can generally use the PreCheck lane when traveling with an enrolled adult, while Global Entry requires each person, including infants, to have their own membership.

Airport timing with and without PreCheck

For an international departure from a large US hub, plan to be at the airport three hours before your flight without PreCheck, and about two and a quarter hours with it. Add 30 minutes if you are checking bags at a peak time, and add another 30 if you are departing on a Friday or during a holiday period. Arriving early costs you a coffee; arriving late costs you the trip.

Two months out: money

Three decisions, none of them complicated.

Get a card with no foreign transaction fee. The standard fee is around three percent of every purchase, which on a $3,000 trip is $90 handed over for nothing. Most travel-oriented credit cards waive it. Apply well before you go so the physical card arrives.

Always choose the local currency. When a card terminal or ATM abroad offers to charge you in dollars, decline. That service, called dynamic currency conversion, applies a poor exchange rate and is essentially a fee dressed as a convenience. Choosing the local currency lets your card network do the conversion at a much better rate.

Get cash from a bank ATM on arrival, not from an exchange counter. Airport exchange bureaus have the worst rates you will encounter. Withdraw a modest amount from a bank-branded ATM once you land, and use cards for everything else. Some countries, including Japan and parts of Germany, remain more cash-dependent than the US; others, like Iceland and Sweden, are nearly cashless.

Tell your bank you are traveling if it still requires that, and carry a second card from a different network, stored separately from the first. A frozen card in a country where you know nobody is a genuinely bad afternoon.

One month out: phone and data

Do not simply turn on your carrier's international day pass and hope. Compare it against the alternatives, because the difference is large.

Most US carriers sell an international plan by the day, priced roughly in the range of $10 to $12 per line per day. On a two-week trip with two travelers that is a meaningful sum. An eSIM data plan purchased from a specialist provider typically costs a fraction of that for the same data allowance, and modern phones support one alongside your existing line, so your US number keeps working for texts and calls.

Whichever you choose, do three things before you leave: download offline maps for the areas you are visiting, download your itinerary as a PDF or print it, and save your lodging addresses in the local language where applicable. A taxi driver who does not read English can read the address on your screen.

Two weeks out: insurance and documents

Travel insurance is genuinely worth buying for a first international trip, and the reason is medical, not baggage. Most US domestic health plans provide limited or no coverage outside the country, and Medicare generally provides none. A policy covering emergency medical treatment and medical evacuation typically runs four to eight percent of your trip cost. Read what it excludes, especially around pre-existing conditions and adventure activities.

Make copies of your passport photo page, your insurance policy number and your credit cards. Keep one set in your luggage separate from the originals and one accessible in cloud storage. Leave a copy with someone at home.

US citizens can also register a trip with the State Department's STEP program, which puts you on the local embassy's contact list for emergencies. It takes five minutes and costs nothing.

Twenty-four hours out: the four things

  1. Check in online and confirm your seat. Airlines occasionally reassign seats, and finding out at the gate is worse than finding out at home.
  2. Verify the departure terminal. Large airports have separate international terminals, and some airlines operate out of more than one.
  3. Weigh your bag. International allowances are often lower than domestic, and overweight fees at the counter are punitive.
  4. Charge everything and pack the adapter in your carry-on. A plug adapter in a checked bag that does not arrive is a small disaster on night one.

On arrival: the first two hours

Follow the signs for passport control and have your passport out. Answer the officer's questions plainly: where you are staying and how long you are visiting. Collect your bag, walk through customs, and only then look for transport.

Take the official taxi rank, a pre-booked transfer, or the airport train. Decline unsolicited offers of rides in the arrivals hall; this is true in most large airports worldwide and is the single most common way new travelers get overcharged.

Then, if at all possible, do very little for the rest of the day. Jet lag after an overnight eastbound flight is real and a first day spent gently outdoors in daylight will reset you faster than a first day spent trying to see four museums. Our planner gives arrival days a lighter schedule by default for exactly this reason, and our destination guides mark which sights are worth the arrival-day energy and which are not.

The thing nobody tells you

The first international trip feels harder than it is. Airports abroad are organized much like airports at home, signage in most tourist destinations is bilingual, and the people you will deal with process confused visitors all day long. The parts that genuinely require preparation are the four covered above, all of which are done before you leave your house.

Everything after that is just a trip.
