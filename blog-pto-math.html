How to get 17 days off using 9 PTO days

Most Americans have between 10 and 20 vacation days a year. Where you place them matters more than how many you have.

By Nadia Brantley Published 03/11/2026 Updated 08/14/2026 11 min read

The United States is the only large wealthy country with no statutory minimum paid vacation. Most full-time workers get somewhere between ten and twenty days, and a meaningful share of those days go unused every year. Against that backdrop, the difference between a good travel year and a frustrating one usually comes down to a scheduling decision made in January, not to a booking decision made in June.

This guide is about placement. It assumes you have a fixed, modest number of days, that you cannot simply request six weeks, and that your employer has a normal approval process. Everything here is legal, ordinary and does not require anyone to bend a policy for you.

The basic arithmetic

A five-day work week gives you two free days on either side of it. Any block of vacation days you take is automatically bracketed by those weekends. Nine consecutive weekdays off, taken as two full work weeks plus one day, becomes seventeen calendar days once you count the weekend before, the weekend in the middle and the weekend after.

Concretely: request the Monday of week one through the Friday of week two, plus the following Monday. You have spent nine PTO days. Your trip runs from the Saturday before through the Sunday nine days later, which is seventeen consecutive days away from work. That extra Monday is doing a lot of work; without it you get sixteen days for eight, which is still an excellent ratio, but the Monday buys you a full recovery day at home before returning.

The same principle scales down. Four PTO days taken Tuesday through Friday, with the Monday being a federal holiday, produces a nine-day break. Two PTO days on a Thursday and Friday produce a four-day weekend that is genuinely long enough for a domestic trip.

Holiday-adjacent scheduling

Eleven federal holidays exist in the United States, and most private employers observe six to eight of them. Each observed holiday that falls on a Monday or Friday is a free day you can build against.

The highest-value holidays for stretching PTO are the ones that already sit adjacent to a weekend. Memorial Day and Labor Day are always Mondays. Independence Day, Veterans Day and Christmas move around the calendar, and in some years land in positions that make a four-day request produce nine days off.

Thanksgiving is the single best stretching opportunity in the American calendar and also the worst travel week of the year. Most employers give Thursday and Friday. Adding Monday, Tuesday and Wednesday of that week gives you nine days off for three PTO days. The catch is that airfare, highway traffic and airport crowding all peak simultaneously. Our honest advice is to use Thanksgiving week for a driving trip within about 400 miles of home, and save the flying for a less contested week.

The quieter version of this trick is the week after a holiday rather than the week containing it. Demand collapses immediately after Labor Day and again in the first two weeks of December. You get the same reduced-price flights that shoulder season offers without competing with everyone else who had the same idea.

A worked example for one year

Fifteen PTO days, placed deliberately, can produce four separate trips totaling 34 days away from work: a nine-day trip in late April using four days, a four-day weekend in June using two days, a seventeen-day trip in late September using nine days, and a four-day weekend at Thanksgiving using zero days beyond the holiday. Same allowance, roughly double the time away compared with taking three scattered single weeks.

The two months most Americans should be traveling in

Late April to mid May, and mid September to late October. These two windows sit either side of the summer peak, and they win on nearly every axis that matters.

Airfare drops noticeably once school is in session. Lodging inventory that is unavailable in July opens up. Popular sites that require timed entry or lottery permits become obtainable rather than theoretical. Weather in most of Europe, Japan and the American West is at its most reliable. And critically, the places themselves are more pleasant: a national park in late September and the same park on the Fourth of July are effectively different destinations.

The trade-off is that if you have school-age children, these windows are unavailable to you. In that case, the best available strategy is to take the first week of summer break rather than the middle, because the crowding curve is not symmetric. The second week of June is materially quieter than the second week of July at nearly every US destination.

Counting travel days honestly

This is where most plans go wrong. A seventeen-day trip is not seventeen days of destination time.

An overnight flight from the East Coast to Europe arrives around 8 AM local time. You will not be functional that day. Plan one light stop and an early night, and treat the day as a half. On the return, a flight leaving Europe at noon reaches the US in the afternoon local time, and that day is gone entirely. On a transpacific route, the losses are larger in both directions.

For a domestic trip the math is kinder but not free. A morning flight plus a rental car pickup plus a drive to your first town consumes most of a day. Our planning rule is to subtract 1.5 days from any international trip and 0.75 days from any domestic one before deciding how many places you can visit.

How to actually get the request approved

Long requests get denied for predictable reasons, and most of them are avoidable.

  • Ask early. Requests submitted three to six months out are approved at much higher rates than requests submitted six weeks out, because your absence can be planned around rather than absorbed.
  • Avoid your team's known crunch. Every team has one: quarter close, an annual audit, a product launch, open enrollment. Asking around it costs you nothing and removes the main objection.
  • Bring the coverage plan with the request. Naming who covers what turns the conversation from a decision into an approval.
  • Split the ask if you must. If nine consecutive days is a hard no, two separate five-day blocks in the same quarter is usually a yes, and produces two nine-day breaks.
  • Book flights after approval, not before. The pressure of a nonrefundable ticket is not a good way to start the conversation, and it will be remembered.

What to do with an unlimited PTO policy

Unlimited policies reliably produce less time off than fixed allocations, because there is no number to use up and no accrual to lose. If you have one, set your own number in January, write it down, and schedule the blocks on the calendar before anything else fills them.

The practical target is between fifteen and eighteen days, placed in the same holiday-adjacent pattern described above. Put the requests in the shared calendar as soon as they are approved. A trip that exists on a calendar in February is far more likely to happen than one that exists only as an intention.

A note on the days you do not use

Rolling days into next year sounds prudent and usually is not. Accrual caps, policy changes and job changes all eat banked time, and the year you were saving for tends not to arrive. If your policy permits carryover, carry over enough to bridge one long trip and use the rest.

The underlying point of all of this is simple. The constraint most Americans face is not money and it is not desire. It is a small number of days, spent badly. Placing them well is free, takes about twenty minutes with a calendar, and is the single highest-return travel decision you will make this year.
