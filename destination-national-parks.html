Five parks, one rental car, and a lot of highway

The Utah and Arizona parks loop is the great American road trip, and the one people most often overpack into too few days. Here is what it costs, when to go, and how far apart everything really is.

  • 7 to 14 days
  • Rental car required
  • About $165 per person per day
See the 3-day plan Plan your own

No passport, no jet lag, no currency conversion.

Overview

Utah's five national parks, usually called the Mighty 5, sit in a rough arc across the southern half of the state: Zion, Bryce Canyon, Capitol Reef, Arches and Canyonlands. Add the Grand Canyon's North or South Rim in Arizona and you have a loop that most people try to do in a week and should really give ten days.

The scale is the thing US travelers underestimate, even those who drive a lot at home. Zion to Arches is 340 miles and takes about five and a half hours with no stops. Doing both in a four-day trip means two of those days are spent looking at asphalt. Our strongest recommendation is to cut a park rather than compress the driving.

The parks have also changed how they manage crowds. Several now require timed entry, shuttle reservations or lottery permits during peak months, and these open on fixed dates months in advance. Missing a reservation window is the single most common way a Southwest trip gets derailed, and it is entirely preventable.

This is a trip that works for a wide range of fitness levels. Every park on the loop has significant scenery accessible from a paved overlook a short walk from a parking lot, alongside strenuous hikes for people who want them.

Fast facts

Fly into
Las Vegas for the western parks, Salt Lake City or Grand Junction for the eastern ones.
Park pass
An America the Beautiful annual pass covers entry to all of them and pays for itself after three parks.
Vehicle
Any standard car handles the paved routes. High clearance is only needed for specific backcountry roads.
Cell service
Unreliable to nonexistent inside the parks. Download offline maps before you go.
Elevation
Bryce Canyon rim sits above 8,000 feet. Expect to feel it on day one.
Time zone
Utah is Mountain Time. Most of Arizona does not observe daylight saving.

The seven-day rule. With seven days including travel from home, pick three parks. With ten, pick five. Adding the Grand Canyon to a Utah loop adds roughly 625 miles and needs two extra days to be worth doing.

Best time to visit

The desert has two comfortable seasons and one genuinely dangerous one.

April to May: the best window

Highs in the 70s and 80s, wildflowers in the lower elevations, and full access to trails. Bryce can still hold snow in early April. Reservation systems are active, so book the moment windows open.

Best for: hiking, first visits, families.

September to October: close second

Summer heat breaks, crowds thin after Labor Day, and October light is superb for photography. Higher-elevation trails can see early snow by late October.

Best for: photographers and anyone avoiding school holidays.

June to August: hot and crowded

Temperatures routinely exceed 100°F in Moab and the inner canyons. Monsoon storms in July and August bring flash flooding. Hiking is limited to before 10 AM and after 5 PM.

If you must: start every hike at sunrise and carry far more water than feels necessary.

November to March: quiet and cold

Very few people, dramatic snow on red rock, and some park roads closed. Bryce is genuinely cold, with highs near freezing. Days are short, so plan fewer stops.

Best for: repeat visitors who want empty overlooks.

Costs people forget

  • One-way rental drop fees, which can exceed $200 between Las Vegas and Salt Lake City.
  • A cooler, camp chairs and a stove if you are camping and flying in.
  • Laundry mid-trip, because desert dust gets into everything.
  • An extra tank of gas; stations are far apart and priced accordingly inside park corridors.

Reservations calendar

Timed entry and permit systems change season to season. As a general rule:

  • Arches timed entry: released a few months ahead, plus a small next-day release
  • Zion Angels Landing permit: seasonal lottery, plus a day-before lottery
  • The Wave at Coyote Buttes: separate advance and daily lotteries
  • In-park campgrounds: six months ahead on recreation.gov, and they sell out in minutes

Always confirm current dates and rules on the official National Park Service and recreation.gov pages before booking anything else.

Getting around

This is a driving trip. There is no rail, and bus service between gateway towns is minimal. Rent the car at the airport and expect to put 1,200 to 1,800 miles on it over ten days.

Shuttles inside the parks

Zion requires a shuttle to reach the main canyon for most of the year; private vehicles are not permitted beyond a certain point. Bryce runs an optional shuttle. Arches uses timed entry rather than a shuttle. Check each park's current system before your dates.

Navigation without service

Cell coverage disappears inside canyons and on long stretches between towns. Download offline maps for the whole region before you leave, and carry a paper map as backup. Do not rely on a navigation app to find a trailhead.

Fuel discipline

Fill up in every gateway town, not when the light comes on. The stretch between Capitol Reef and Moab has long gaps, and prices inside park corridors run well above the interstate average.

Sunrise and sunset are the trip

Midday in the desert is flat light and high heat. Build days around the two golden hours, and treat the middle of the day as driving, eating and resting time. This is not a preference; it is how the landscape works.

Where to stay

Springdale for Zion. Walkable to the park entrance and the shuttle.

Bryce Canyon City or Tropic for Bryce. Tropic is cheaper and 20 minutes out.

Torrey for Capitol Reef. Small, quiet, and the only real option.

Moab for Arches and Canyonlands. Most lodging and food on the whole loop.

In-park lodges at Zion and Bryce book roughly 13 months ahead and are worth setting a calendar reminder for.

Safety notes

Search and rescue call-outs in these parks are overwhelmingly heat, water and navigation, not wildlife.

Water

One gallon per person per day is the minimum in summer, and more if you are hiking. Carry it in the car as well as on the trail. Dehydration is the number one reason people need help here.

Heat timing

Rangers close some trails to daytime hiking in summer for good reason. If your hike will not be finished by 10 AM, start earlier or move it to the evening.

Flash floods

July through September, storms miles away can fill a slot canyon in minutes. Check the daily flash flood potential rating at any visitor center and take it seriously.

Drop-offs

Most overlooks have no railings. Falls are the leading cause of death in Grand Canyon and a regular occurrence in Canyonlands. Keep children within arm's reach.

Cryptobiotic soil

The dark crusty ground between plants is living soil that takes decades to regrow. Stay on trails and slickrock. It matters, and rangers will tell you the same.

If something goes wrong

Call 911 where you have service. Many trailheads have emergency call boxes. A satellite messenger is genuinely worthwhile for anyone hiking alone.

Always leave your route and expected return time with someone not on the trip.
